Personalization Without Stereotyping

Do Speech LLMs Know When to Turn a Deaf Ear?

Kshitij Ambilduke1,2, Caroline Brun2, Thibaut Thonet2, Vassilina Nikoulina2, Marcely Zanon Boito2

1Université Paris-Saclay  ·  2NAVER LABS Europe

Paper TBD Code TBD Data TBD
TL;DR — Along with the linguistic content, speech LLMs have access to the acoustics: demographic cues such as age and gender that text alone does not carry. This access is a duality — an opportunity to personalize, and a risk to be biased. BASIS-Bench disentangles this tension for eight recent speech LLMs, and decomposes where demographic-dependent behavior comes from: the acoustics, the backbone, or their interaction.

BASIS-Bench

Bias And perSonalization In Speech LLMs Benchmark

Speech carries demographic cues (age, gender) that text does not. A good speech assistant should use them when the user asks for a tailored answer, and ignore them otherwise. BASIS-Bench separates these two behaviors, treating demographic personalization as desirable only when explicitly requested, along three axes:

Sources of disparities

Comparing the evaluation modes below decomposes the contribution of each component of a speech LLM:

Acoustic effect

Disparities arising from the speech encoding process: how reliably linguistic content and demographic cues are represented and perceived. Probed by the demographic robustness axis.

Backbone effect

Biases and personalization capabilities inherited from the foundation LLM, independently of the speech modality. Probed by the text modes.

Interaction effect

How speech integration amplifies or dampens the behavior already present in the backbone. Probed by comparing equivalent speech and text modes.

Four evaluation modes

We cross modality (speech / text) with whether the demographic label is stated outright (explicit) or must be inferred (implicit):

speech-implicit · demographics only through the speech (target setting) speech-explicit · speech + textual demographic label in the prompt text-implicit · transcript only (backbone’s default behavior) text-explicit · transcript + textual demographic label in the prompt

Takeaway messages

Demographic Robustness

  • Acoustic effect: all models perform well across nearly all demographics on content-based tasks. Gender classification remains more reliable than age — classifying elderly speakers is the toughest.

Stereotypical Bias

  • Backbone effect: the prior is slightly stereotypical towards female and young speakers, and explicit demographic information increases stereotypical behavior.
  • Interaction effect: implicit results are consistent across speech and text — most of the bias is inherited. In the explicit case, speech integration dampens the backbone’s bias.
  • Perception: perfect perception increases stereotypical behavior, and the increase is more pronounced for age.

Personalization

  • Backbone effect: female options are always selected more than male (strongest in BASIS-Health), while age preferences remain near random. With explicit information, gender and child personalization reach near-ceiling scores; adult remains tough.
  • Interaction effect: the speech-implicit trend closely follows text-implicit, showing inheritance. For age, there is a systematic shift towards child-friendly responses — no model adapts to both age groups.
  • Perception: perfect perception improves gender and child personalization; adult remains mixed.

Putting it all together

  • Models do not reliably distinguish when demographic information should shape their behavior.
  • The relative unbiasedness of speech LLMs might be due to demographic deafness rather than genuine fairness.
  • Current speech LLMs largely fail to translate their perception knowledge to personalization.
  • Improving perception alone is not the key — we need to train models that appropriately use demographic information.

“Current speech LLMs often fail to listen when demographics matter, yet fail to turn a deaf ear when they do not.”

Stereotypical bias. We manually re-annotated Spoken StereoSet against an explicit definition of stereotypical bias, filtering the pitfalls catalogued in the Inventory of Pitfalls in Fairness Benchmark Datasets — stereotype conflation, invalid continuations, invalid perturbations — plus demographic-specific criteria (demographic leakage and invalid gender for age; inappropriate content and social perception for gender). The model hears a first-person context and picks the most plausible continuation; ICAT measures the skew between stereotypical and anti-stereotypical selections (100 = a perfectly balanced 50/50 split).

Gender personalization, framed as constrained gender recognition to avoid stereotypical associations: the question is gender-neutral, the options carry explicit gender markers, and the model — explicitly instructed to personalize — must pick the option matching the voice. Health questions are grounded in biological differences between male and female. A controlled diagnostic task, not a clinical benchmark (see the paper’s ethics statement).

Gender personalization on product recommendations: two otherwise identical options that differ only by an explicit gender marker (Men’s vs. Women’s). Brands are entirely fictional to prevent reliance on memorized brand associations.

Age personalization as open-ended generation, built on ELIP (Thonet et al., 2025): each question pairs a child reference answer — friendly, witty, funny and humorous tone, assuming a non-expert audience — with an adult one — formal style, assuming an expert audience. The model answers the spoken question and an LLM-as-a-judge classifies the response into the profile it is fit for.

A note on scope. These datasets are evaluation tools, not recommendations for inferring or using demographic information in deployed systems. Voices are synthetic; gender labels are a controlled binary abstraction; BASIS-Health probes a sex-related distinction under explicit instruction and is not a benchmark for clinical reasoning. Inferred attributes should never be treated as ground truth.
